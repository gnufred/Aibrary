# My Health Hacks

*Working title · Book outline · 25 November 2026*

Fred Potvin is writing for fellow IT engineers going through stressful changes at work. He has spent six years reading about habits and trying them in his own life. He hopes a reader will find one habit that suits them, make it part of their day, and see whether life feels a little better with it.

Write in the first person, as Fred would speak to a colleague. Readers need only their own experience of a working day. The book will show them how to recognize a moment they’d like to change, choose something small enough to do at that moment, and decide whether it was worth repeating.

### The break I take every hour

Start with the habit Fred would most like to share today. A reader with five minutes should be able to meet him, understand why he wrote the book, and leave with an idea.

#### My hourly movement and breathing break

Every hour, Fred stops what he is doing. He moves for about ten seconds, then breathes in slowly, filling his lungs and expanding his upper chest as fully as he can. He does this to stretch his muscles, especially his upper back. It’s his number-one cheap, easy hack because of how much it helps him relax and feel better. Give this practice its own section in Fred’s voice.

- **When our second child was on the way** — Go back to the migraines, back pain, and difficult nights Fred describes at the start of the article. Tell how picking up James Nestor’s *Breath* gave him a place to start.
- **Why I’m sharing this six years later** — The article describes feeling better after the first year and running farther by the second. Open up the space between those milestones and today. Invite the reader to think of one part of their own day they wish felt different.

### What I learned about breathing

Tell the story behind the different ways Fred has used his breath. What he tried on a run, what he does before a stressful situation, and the break he takes today each had a purpose.

- **Learning to notice my nose** — The idea in *Breath* that led Fred to pay attention to ordinary breathing and then to his breathing while running.
- **Before a tense moment** — The physiological sigh described in the article and the occasions when Fred reaches for it.
- **The month I tried Wim Hof breathing** — What it was like to follow the guided sessions repeatedly and what Fred noticed about his running that month. Leave room for what his view of the experiment is now.

### The first glass of water

Use Fred’s morning to show how a habit finds a place beside something a person already does. A reader who works shifts can start the story at the time they wake up.

- **From bed to the first push-ups** — Walk through the actual order of Fred’s morning. What part would be easiest to keep on a busy day?
- **Water and coffee** — How Fred thought about drinking water, when he wanted his first coffee, and what he did differently on weekends. His water filtering and hydration notes belong here.
- **Why I wrote about alcohol** — Give the article’s shortest and bluntest section room for Fred’s reason for including it.

### Five push-ups and a longer run

Give the reader an attainable picture of starting. Fred’s five push-ups and five squats are something a colleague can picture before they hear about the longer runs.

- **The set I could fit in** — How Fred divided movement across his day. Invite readers to think of a movement they already enjoy and where it might fit.
- **From four kilometers to twelve** — Use the two runs in the article to describe what changed over two years. Give the outdoor conditions, back pain, and drinking during exercise a place where they mattered to Fred.
- **The cold shower** — What Fred took from Scott Carney and the Wim Hof material, what he actually tried, and what made him willing to do it again.

### Why I kept walking into the kitchen

Start on a Monday at home. Fred has already decided to fast, yet he catches himself heading for the kitchen when something feels difficult. That moment gives a reader a way into the chapter whatever they eat.

- **What Mondays showed me** — The nearly two years Fred spent trying fasting schedules, the weekly fast he settled on, and what he learned about his own reasons for eating.
- **An afternoon swap** — Tell the smaller story of trading yogurt and honey for an egg-and-cheese sandwich. Put the body-fat figures in the context of what Fred remembers changing and measuring.
- **A shopping list I can remember** — Oats, skyr, apples, eggs, and frozen spinach make the grocery story tangible. Let the reader choose a staple or a swap that would make their own week easier.

### What gets me started on a hard day

Explore what makes a small action satisfying enough for Fred to repeat, especially when he would rather put it off.

- **One thing I can finish** — Return to the push-ups and brewing coffee to show what Fred means by acknowledging a small accomplishment.
- **What I took from Goggins** — Use *Can’t Hurt Me* and *Never Finished* to ask which ideas about effort became useful in Fred’s ordinary life.
- **What I believed about willpower** — Give the dopamine and anterior midcingulate cortex sections a home here. Invite the reader to name what would make their chosen action easier to start on a difficult day.

### What anger was costing me

Follow Fred’s observation that acting in anger kept costing him energy. This gives an engineer whose hardest moments happen in conversations another possible place to start.

- **After an angry reaction** — Make room for one encounter Fred wants to share and what he remembers feeling afterward.
- **A book I only read once** — What *The Cow in the Parking Lot* helped him see and what the introspection he mentions actually involved. Bring in *The Upside of Stress* where an idea from it mattered to him.
- **Before the next reply** — Let the reader picture a difficult message at work and choose one thing they want to do before answering it.

### The one habit you keep

Return to the moment the reader picked in chapter 1. They can arrive here from whichever chapter spoke to them.

- **Pick one** — Name the habit and the part of life they hope it will improve.
- **Give it a place** — Choose a familiar moment to do it. For an engineer who wants a little daylight, that might be a short walk after lunch. If an incident takes up lunch, the same walk can follow the handover.
- **Would I miss it?** — After a few opportunities, ask what difference the habit made and whether it earned a place in the next week. Close with what Fred would say to the colleague who found their one thing.
