# My Health Hacks

*Working title · Book plan, 25 November 2026*

## Who Fred wants to reach

Fred Potvin wants to hand this book to a fellow IT engineer going through a stressful period of change. Imagine trying to learn a new system while people still depend on you to keep the old one running. What part of your day would you most like to feel a little better? Fred has spent six years researching and trying things in his own life. His hope is that somewhere in those years there is **one habit you can make your own**.

Write a short, first-person book in English, with the candor of a colleague sharing something that helped. Open with the cheap, easy practice Fred values most today. Then tell the reader why he began, what he found in the books and recordings he followed, and what happened when he tried those ideas. Give space to the awkward parts, such as catching himself on the way to the kitchen on a Monday when he had decided to fast.

Readers bring knowledge of their own working day. The book supplies the rest, including what a cue is, how to choose an action small enough to repeat, and how to tell what a personal observation or a study can establish. Examples should work on paper, on a shift, or at home with whatever time, food, and space the reader already has.

### What the reader takes away

- Name one part of life they want to improve and what a welcome change there would feel like.
- Choose a habit that fits their circumstances and explain what drew them to it in Fred’s account.
- Connect it to a moment they recognize, make room for it on a disrupted day, and pick it up again after missing it.
- Use their own experience to decide whether to keep, adapt, or set it aside. Feeling more at ease before the next conversation is a worthwhile outcome in its own right.

### The break I take every hour

Let a reader meet Fred through something he does today. Use the passing of an hour to explain a cue, something familiar that reminds him to act. Then show what he does and why it feels worth doing again.

#### My hourly movement and breathing break

Every hour, Fred stops what he is doing, moves for about ten seconds, then breathes in slowly, filling his lungs and expanding his upper chest as fully as he can. He does it to stretch his muscles, especially his upper back. He calls this his number-one cheap, easy hack. He says it helps him relax a great deal and feel better. Fred supplied this account for the book on 25 November 2026. Give his practice a section of its own in his voice.

#### When our second child was on the way

Rewind to Fred feeling unwell as his family was growing, picking up James Nestor’s *Breath*, and seeing changes in his first year. His running comparison belongs to a two-year span. Six years describes the whole period of research and personal testing. Fred singled out nasal breathing as the most important change he made when he wrote the article. Ask how his hourly practice took shape over the years. [Opening of the original article][opening]

#### Where in your day would a change matter?

Invite the reader to think of one familiar moment, perhaps finishing a long call with their upper back feeling tight or closing a laptop still angry about a conversation. Let them carry that moment through the book. Someone who already has a habit in mind can turn straight to [the closing exercise](#the-one-habit-you-keep).

For research, ask Fred what first prompted the hourly break and which details of that first year he wants to share. Attribute relief from symptoms to his recollection and establish the chronology before connecting events. British occupational guidance offers context for changes of activity during screen work. Fred is the source for his particular sequence and how it feels to him. [HSE guidance on screen-work breaks](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/dse/work-routine.htm)

### What I learned about breathing

Build on the hourly break by showing why Fred has tried several kinds of breathing. The reader should be able to tell which practice he used for which purpose.

- **The book that made me notice my nose** — Follow the idea in *Breath* that caught his attention. Move from ordinary breathing to what he tried on runs. Locate the passages on exercise intensity and adult facial structure before deciding how much anatomy this story needs.
- **Before a stressful moment** — Describe when Fred uses a physiological sigh. Set his brief use alongside what researchers actually asked participants to do in a study of repeated cyclic sighing. Compare duration, setting, and the outcome they measured.
- **A month of guided breathing** — Revisit the Wim Hof sessions and the 15% increase in running speed Fred reported that month. Find out how he measured it and what else was changing in his training. Keep this episode distinct from both the sigh and the hourly stretch.

Trace Nestor’s account, the Galpin interview, the Huberman recording, and the two guided sessions through the article links. Check evidence and safety for each specific method before proposing any reader exercise. A reader can also use this chapter simply to notice when they want a pause. [Breathing sections and original links][breathing] · [Balban and colleagues’ breathing study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)

### The first glass of water

Use the cue introduced in chapter 1 to find one choice a reader already makes every day. Waking up gives the example a place in the day even when a shift changes.

- **Before the coffee** — Follow Fred from getting out of bed to water and push-ups. Let the reader pick out a single action that fits their morning.
- **What goes in the glass** — Tell the story behind Fred’s water and coffee habits. Ask what delaying his first cup or changing his weekend routine did for him. Explain serving size and caffeine dose if they help the reader understand the example.
- **Why I wrote about alcohol** — Give Fred room to explain his view of alcohol. Establish what he actually does and what he wants a colleague to understand about that choice.

Follow the drinking-water, Galpin, caffeine, and alcohol recordings. Check what local water authorities say about a named contaminant and treatment before using the jar or filter example. Check evidence for the proposed coffee window and for hydration formulas. Distinguish water during an ordinary day from exercise-related fluid losses in chapter 4. Verify any public-health advice about alcohol, including guidance for people at risk of withdrawal. [Water, coffee, waking up, and alcohol in the article][drinks] · [EFSA’s caffeine opinion](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4102)

### Five push-ups and a longer run

Show how Fred found room to move. Start with a movement that fit his day, then teach the difference between how often someone moves, how hard they work, and what they can measure afterward.

- **The set I could fit in** — Reconstruct his first sets of push-ups and squats. Invite the reader to choose a familiar movement they enjoy and one occasion where it would fit, indoors or outside.
- **Four kilometers and twelve** — Put his 4 km in 45 minutes and later 12 km in an hour side by side. Explain pace using those two runs. Bring in outdoor training and hydration through the actual conditions Fred remembers.
- **When my back hurt** — Return to the back pain in the opening. Ask what happened on a particular occasion and what Fred took from the linked exercise video. Put any reader-facing advice in the context of guidance for the kind of pain it addresses.

Check the run dates, conditions, and pace arithmetic where records exist. The one-month figure in chapter 2 needs its own comparison. Follow Galpin on intervals and fluid replacement, then check the claims about daily intensity, training outdoors in harsh weather, and double-counting fluids already consumed. Compare the back exercise with current low-back-pain guidance. [Fred’s training account][training] · [Back-pain video in the article][back] · [WHO physical activity guidelines](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128)

### Why I kept walking into the kitchen

Follow a work-from-home moment the reader may recognize. Use the idea of a cue to examine what draws someone to the kitchen and how a small grocery decision can make the next meal easier.

- **The afternoon snack** — Start with Fred’s yogurt and honey and the egg-and-cheese sandwich he swapped in. Describe what he wanted from the change. Introduce protein, portions, or measurement uncertainty only where they explain this example.
- **A shopping list I knew by heart** — Build one ordinary meal from Fred’s staples, such as oats, skyr, apples, and cinnamon, once he confirms how he combines them. Ask what became cheaper or easier. Invite readers to find one staple that suits their own budget and tastes.
- **What I noticed on Mondays** — After nearly two years of trying different fasting schedules, Fred found that a weekly 36-hour fast suited him. The detail to linger on is catching himself walking into the kitchen after a stressful moment. A reader can notice the same sort of cue while eating their usual meals.

Trace the four nutrition videos and Thomas DeLauer’s fasting video to the sources behind their claims. Check how Fred obtained the 25% and 19% body-fat readings eight weeks apart, what else changed, and the uncertainty of that measurement. Verify the populations, fasting schedules, outcomes, and risks in any study cited. Keep Fred’s weekly fast as personal history and the reader exercise about an everyday food choice. [Food, nutrition, fasting, and their original sources][food]

### What gets me started on a hard day

Use the first small action from chapter 1 to ask what makes Fred willing to begin something difficult. Give the reader a way to make the start of their chosen habit easier.

- **One thing I can finish** — Follow the satisfaction Fred describes after a small task. Ask for one step he has used on a difficult workday and what acknowledging it changed for him.
- **The appeal of doing something hard** — Read *Can’t Hurt Me* and *Never Finished* for the ideas Fred took from David Goggins. Examine the article’s dopamine and anterior midcingulate cortex explanations. Define either term only if a supported explanation adds something to the story.
- **The cold shower** — Bring Scott Carney’s *What Doesn’t Kill Us* and the Wim Hof material back to Fred’s experience of choosing a cold shower. Ask what made the discomfort worth it to him, and whether he still chooses it.

Trace the two motivation recordings to human studies. Check what researchers measured before attributing a brain change to completing tasks or exercising willpower. For cold exposure, distinguish showers from immersion and days feeling sick from days absent from work. Keep the reader’s practical choice at the scale of one manageable task. [Motivation sources][motivation] · [Fred’s cold-shower account][cold] · [Buijze and colleagues’ shower trial](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0161749)

### What anger was costing me

Return to the stressful moment the reader named at the start. Help them recognize an opportunity to choose a response when another person is involved.

- **How long anger stayed with me** — Fred writes that acting in anger used up energy he wanted for other things. Ask him for one interaction he is comfortable recounting and how he felt afterward.
- **A book I only read once** — Follow what Fred took from *The Cow in the Parking Lot* and the introspection he says it required. Bring in Kelly McGonigal’s *The Upside of Stress* where a particular passage changed his understanding.
- **Before pressing Send** — Use an explicitly fictional example of a tense engineering handover. Give the reader time to choose what they want from their next reply. Leave room for the conversation about workload or expectations that may still need to happen.

Locate the relevant passages in the editions Fred read, starting with Leonard Scheff and Susan Edmiston’s book. Check any broader claims about stress against evidence for the kind and duration of stress described. [Anger and reading notes in the article][books] · [Publisher’s listing for *The Cow in the Parking Lot*](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/susan-edmiston/the-cow-in-the-parking-lot-a-zen-approach-to-overcoming-anger/9780761158158/) · [WHO guidelines on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240053052)

### The one habit you keep

Bring the reader back to the part of their day they wanted to improve. They can finish this chapter with a choice from any earlier chapter, or with an idea of their own.

- **Choose the moment** — Name one thing that would make the day better and one small action that might help. Choose a cue within reach, such as getting up, finishing lunch, or handing over a shift.
- **Make room on a messy day** — An engineer who wants a little daylight chooses a short walk after lunch. When an incident runs through lunch, the same walk can follow the handover. If both chances pass, lunch tomorrow is another opportunity.
- **Decide what earned its place** — After a few opportunities, the engineer asks whether they got outside on days they would have spent indoors and whether that felt worth keeping. Invite readers to ask the equivalent question about their own habit. Close with Fred’s reason for passing his experience on to a colleague.

Walk the example through an ordinary and an interrupted workday on paper. Check that someone can complete it with one action, their own cue, and an observation they care about. Look up the population, range of outcomes, and observation period in habit-formation research before making claims about how long change takes. [Lally and colleagues’ habit-formation study](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

## Decisions to carry into research

- **Voice and length** — Plan for a short book in Fred’s voice with eight chapters. Keep *My Health Hacks* as a working title. The amount of new material depends on the stories and sources Fred wants to contribute.
- **What happened across the six years** — Ask which habits endured, which he changed or dropped, and what he remembers about the stretch between the early milestones and today. The outline groups the middle chapters by subject. Only date an event when Fred or a record can place it.
- **Depth of the health material** — Practical examples center on ordinary choices at work and home. Pain treatment, disease management, intensive breathwork, fasting schedules, and cold-exposure protocols fall outside the reader exercises. End with a brief reading guide identifying which idea Fred took from each book. Give Philipp Dettmer’s *Immune*, which he reports reading three times, a place there. [Fred’s bookshelf][books]
- **Author’s choices before publication** — Fred decides how much family and medical history to share and which license to give his own manuscript. Carry his book notice and the credits for any cleared third-party material into both editions.
